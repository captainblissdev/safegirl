# Knowledge Base — STIs

## KB-S3
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** I might have been exposed to HIV. Is there something I can take to stop it?
- **Answer:** Yes — post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is a course of medication started as soon as possible after exposure. Both Kenyan and WHO guidance agree it should be started within 72 hours, since it becomes less effective the longer you wait.
- **Evidence/claim:** PEP (28-day ARV regimen) must be initiated within 72 hours of exposure per both sources — this is one of the few points of full agreement between MoH and WHO.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH YFS Guidelines (2005), Annex 1, pp. 26–28 and WHO (2018), Ch. 7 §7.3.5/7.3.14, pp. 43, 46
- **Conditions/limitations:** Specific drug names/dosing not established by sources — do not include in the answer.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Must direct to immediate facility-based care — this is time-critical.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S4
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** I have unusual discharge, itching, burning or lower belly pain. Could it be an STI?
- **Answer:** It could be, but unusual discharge can have other causes too, and only a test can tell which. Many STIs cause no symptoms at all. Kenya's 2026 guidelines now prefer lab tests that find the exact infection before treatment, so ask for a test at a health facility. Lower belly pain, pain during sex or bleeding between periods needs a visit soon, because untreated infections can spread to the womb and tubes.
- **Evidence/claim:** Laboratory-confirmed (aetiological) diagnosis is the preferred approach to STI care in Kenya. Chlamydia symptoms in women include abnormal vaginal discharge, pain on passing urine, and bleeding after sex or between periods; gonorrhoea in women may cause abnormal discharge and lower abdominal discomfort. Untreated infection can ascend to cause pelvic inflammatory disease, ectopic pregnancy and infertility.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 3.3.1 p. 161; s. 3.5.1 Chlamydia p. 171; s. 3.5.2 Gonorrhoea p. 171; WHO, Sexually transmitted infections fact sheet
- **Conditions/limitations:** Where a facility has no laboratory, the guideline still allows clinical diagnosis (s. 3.3.2 p. 162). No treatment or drug advice is given. The 2018 guideline (the earlier source) was replaced by the 2026 edition; the privacy sentence that came from it is dropped.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Lower belly pain with fever, or pain during sex, needs a clinic visit soon. She should not self-treat.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S5
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** Can STIs be cured, and can they harm me if I leave them?
- **Answer:** Four common STIs (syphilis, gonorrhoea, chlamydia and trichomoniasis) can be cured with antibiotics. HIV and herpes cannot be cured, but medicine can keep them under control. Some untreated infections can spread and cause pelvic inflammatory disease, so it is better to get tested and treated early.
- **Evidence/claim:** WHO: syphilis, gonorrhoea, chlamydia and trichomoniasis are curable; HIV and herpes are not but can be managed. Kenya 2026: untreated chlamydia and gonorrhoea can ascend to cause PID, ectopic pregnancy and infertility; testing finds the exact infection.
- **Source:** WHO, Sexually transmitted infections fact sheet; Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 3.3.1 p. 161; s. 3.5.1 and 3.5.2 p. 171
- **Conditions/limitations:** The curable and not-curable statements rest on the WHO fact sheet only; IG2026 pages read do not state curability. Being cured does not protect against catching the infection again. Partner treatment is not included. The 2018 guideline citation is dropped.
- **Safety/referral notes:** She should go to a clinic for testing and treatment rather than self-treating.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S6
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** Is the HPV vaccine available to me, and am I old enough?
- **Answer:** Kenya's 2026 HIV and STI guideline says the HPV vaccine is available in Kenya for girls aged 9 to 14, and since November 2025 it is given as a single dose. If you are older than that, ask at a government health facility whether you can still get it.
- **Evidence/claim:** Kenya's HPV programme targets 10-year-old girls and moved from a two-dose to a single-dose schedule in November 2025. IG2026 (p. 182) adds that the HPV vaccine is highly effective for preventing genital warts, but does not state who is eligible.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 1.5.2.5, p. 57; WHO AFRO, Kenya transitions to single-dose HPV vaccination schedule (page dated 1 November 2025)
- **Conditions/limitations:** Sources disagree on the target age (WHO AFRO: 10-year-olds; news reports: 10 to 14). Whether it is free and whether catch-up is offered for older girls is not stated in any primary source seen, including IG2026 and FP7.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Do not tell a girl over 14 she is ineligible. She should ask at a clinic.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S7
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** Can I take PrEP to prevent HIV? I'm a teenager.
- **Answer:** PrEP is medicine that HIV-negative people can use to lower their risk of getting HIV. Kenya's 2026 guidelines say it is not for adolescents under 15. From age 15, a health worker can check whether it suits you. You need an HIV test first. There are now several forms, including a daily pill, a vaginal ring, and injections given every 2 or every 6 months, and a health worker will help you choose. Ask about condoms too, because PrEP does not protect against other STIs.
- **Evidence/claim:** PrEP is indicated for HIV-negative people at ongoing risk; "Adolescents under 15 years" is listed as a contraindication; eligibility requires a confirmed HIV-negative test; options are daily oral PrEP, the dapivirine vaginal ring (every 28 days), cabotegravir long-acting injection (every 8 weeks) and lenacapavir injection (every 26 weeks).
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 1.3.1 pp. 17 to 18; s. 1.3.3 p. 19; Table 1.15 p. 21; WHO, HIV fact sheet
- **Conditions/limitations:** Not every facility offers every form. Some forms have extra conditions (kidney or liver checks). The pages read do not state the consent rule for 15- to 17-year-olds (Table 1.12 p. 17 says "national consent guidance"). "PrEP does not protect against other STIs" is not stated on the pages read; keep the WHO citation or remove the sentence.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Girls under 15 who are at risk should go to a clinic or youth-friendly service for other prevention options. If she may have been exposed in the last 3 days she needs PEP urgently, not PrEP.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S8
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** Can I have an STI without knowing?
- **Answer:** Yes. Many STIs cause no symptoms. For example, chlamydia causes no symptoms in about 7 in 10 women, and gonorrhoea is also often symptom-free in women. If they are not treated, they can spread to the womb and tubes and later cause pain, a pregnancy outside the womb, or trouble getting pregnant. The only way to know is a test at a health facility.
- **Evidence/claim:** Genital chlamydia infection is asymptomatic in about 70% of women; gonorrhoea is often asymptomatic in women (fewer than half of infected women report symptoms); untreated infections may cause PID, ectopic pregnancy and tubal infertility; laboratory testing also detects asymptomatic infection.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 3.5.1 p. 171; s. 3.5.2 p. 171; s. 3.3.1 p. 161
- **Conditions/limitations:** The 70% figure is the guideline's figure for chlamydia in women. It is not given for other STIs. Not every untreated infection causes complications.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Anyone who has had sex without a condom, or whose partner may have an STI, can ask for a test at a clinic. She does not have to have symptoms.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S9
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** If a person with HIV takes their medicine, can they still pass HIV to a partner during sex?
- **Answer:** Not if their viral load stays undetectable. Kenya's 2026 guidelines say that people whose blood tests show an undetectable viral load on two tests six months apart have effectively no risk of passing HIV on through sex. This is called U=U: undetectable equals untransmittable. It depends on carrying on with the medicine and the clinic's viral load tests. It does not apply in pregnancy and breastfeeding, to injecting drug use, or to needle-stick injuries.
- **Evidence/claim:** Definition: a durable undetectable viral load (two consecutive results below 200 copies/ml six months apart) means effectively no risk of sexual transmission. U=U messaging is not applicable in pregnant and breastfeeding women living with HIV, people who inject drugs, or needle-stick injuries.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 1.6.5 p. 97
- **Conditions/limitations:** This is about HIV only; it says nothing about other STIs. A person cannot tell her partner's viral load without the partner's own results.
- **Safety/referral notes:** A partner of someone with HIV can ask a clinic about testing and prevention (PrEP or PEP). For other STIs, a test is still the only way to know.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S10
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** Is vaginal discharge always a sign of an STI?
- **Answer:** No. Bacterial vaginosis, the most common cause of vaginal discharge in women of childbearing age, is not an STI, and neither is a yeast infection (thrush). Trichomoniasis is an STI that can cause a yellow or greenish, sometimes frothy, discharge and itching, and many women who have it have no symptoms. Because these can look alike, you can't tell by yourself. A health worker can test you and tell you which it is.
- **Evidence/claim:** BV is the most common cause of vaginal discharge in women of childbearing age and is not an STI. Candidiasis is not an STI. About 50 to 85% of women with T. vaginalis may have no symptoms; symptomatic women usually have abnormal discharge, and about half have vulval itching, with a yellow or greenish, sometimes frothy discharge.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 3.5.4 p. 172; s. 3.5.5 p. 172; s. 3.5.6 p. 173
- **Conditions/limitations:** No description of normal discharge is given because none was found in the pages read. No treatments are given. This overlaps with the "discharge" gap topic, so the gynaecologist should approve it as the answer or replace it.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Lower belly pain, fever or pain during sex needs a visit soon. A girl who may be pregnant should be seen before taking any treatment.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S11
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** How do I get tested for HIV, and can I test myself?
- **Answer:** You can be tested at a health facility, and a health worker should ask for your consent first. Kenya's guidelines also approve HIV self-test kits, where you collect your own sample (from your mouth or a drop of blood) and read the result yourself. If a self-test shows a positive result, it must be confirmed at a health facility with the national tests. If you test negative, a health worker can talk to you about ways to stay protected.
- **Evidence/claim:** HIV testing is offered with informed consent. HIV self-testing uses Ministry of Health-approved kits (oral or blood-based) and is particularly effective for reaching adolescents; a reactive self-test must be confirmed through the national testing algorithm. HIV-negative clients are linked to prevention services.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 1.1.1 p. 1; s. 1.1.3 p. 2
- **Conditions/limitations:** The pages read do not state where to buy self-test kits, what they cost, or any age limit; no cost or "free" claim is made. A recent exposure can be missed by an early test (see NEW-S-10).
- **Safety/referral notes:** If a self-test is reactive, she should go to a facility for confirmation and support, not decide on her own. A recent exposure within 72 hours needs PEP urgently (see NEW-S-06).
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S12
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** How soon after sex can an HIV test tell me if I have HIV?
- **Answer:** Not straight away. After HIV gets into the body there is a "window period" before a test can detect it, and different tests detect it at different times, so an early negative result may not be reliable. A health worker will tell you when to test again. If a result is unclear, Kenya's guidelines say to test again after 14 days. After a possible exposure, check-up tests are repeated at 28 days and at 12 weeks.
- **Evidence/claim:** The window period is the time between exposure and detectable antibodies; different tests detect at different stages. Inconclusive results are retested after 14 days. After PEP, HIV testing is repeated at 28 days and 12 weeks; blood or organ donation is avoided until confirmed HIV-negative at 12 weeks.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 1.1.7 Table 1.6 p. 12; s. 1.2.4 p. 16; s. 1.2.5 p. 16
- **Conditions/limitations:** No single number of days is given for when a test becomes reliable. The 28-day and 12-week schedule is the PEP follow-up schedule, not a general rule for all tests.
- **Safety/referral notes:** A possible exposure within the last 3 days is urgent: go to a facility about PEP (see NEW-S-06). Use condoms until the result is confirmed.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S13
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** What is pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)?
- **Answer:** PID is an infection of the womb, tubes and the area around them. It is usually caused by STIs that were not treated, such as chlamydia and gonorrhoea. The signs can be mild or missing, so it is often missed. Pelvic or lower belly pain is the main sign, and fever or unusual discharge can come with it. Treatment that is delayed can leave lasting damage to the tubes and make it harder to get pregnant. See a health worker soon if you have lower belly pain, and go to hospital urgently for severe pelvic pain or a high fever.
- **Evidence/claim:** PID is an inflammatory disorder of the upper genital tract commonly caused by untreated chlamydia, gonorrhoea, trichomonas and Mycoplasma genitalium; symptoms can be subtle or absent; delay contributes to complications; diagnosis is based on pelvic or abdominal pain with tenderness, and fever and purulent discharge add to the picture; severe pelvic pain or high fever (above 38 degrees) are criteria for hospital referral. Complications include ectopic pregnancy and tubal infertility.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 3.6 pp. 178-179; s. 3.5.1 p. 171
- **Conditions/limitations:** Treatment details are not included. The referral list in the guideline is for clinicians; the "go to hospital urgently" wording here is a simplified version for lay readers.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Severe pelvic pain can also be an ectopic pregnancy or appendicitis, so it should never be treated at home.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S14
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** What are genital warts, and can I prevent them?
- **Answer:** Genital warts are small growths on or around the genitals or the anus, caused mostly by two types of HPV (types 6 and 11). Some are visible and some are inside the vagina or anus. They can pass to a partner, and condoms help reduce the spread. A health worker should look at any new growth, because other conditions can look similar. The HPV vaccine is very effective at preventing genital warts. HPV that causes no signs usually clears by itself.
- **Evidence/claim:** Anogenital warts are largely caused by HPV genotypes 6 and 11; may be internal; highly infectious to partners, and condoms help reduce transmission; examination is needed to rule out other causes; subclinical genital HPV infection typically clears spontaneously; the quadrivalent or nonavalent HPV vaccine is highly effective for preventing anogenital warts.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 3.9 p. 182
- **Conditions/limitations:** No treatments are given. Whether and where a girl can get the vaccine is not stated here (see NEW-S-03, still unverified).
- **Safety/referral notes:** She should see a health worker for any growth rather than trying home treatments.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S15
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** I have painful sores or blisters on my genitals. Could it be herpes or syphilis?
- **Answer:** It could be. Painful blisters or sores, with itching or burning when you pass urine, can be genital herpes, which can come back. Syphilis and other infections can also cause genital sores, and sometimes more than one infection is present. Some sores are not from STIs at all, for example a yeast infection or an injury. A health worker can test you and tell you which it is, so go to a clinic rather than treating it yourself.
- **Evidence/claim:** Genital ulcers can have more than one infectious cause (for example herpes and syphilis); genital herpes presents with painful vesicles or ulcers, itching, burning, dysuria and recurrent outbreaks; some genital lesions are not sexually transmitted (yeast, trauma and others); diagnosis uses tests such as PCR.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 3.4 and Table 3.8 p. 164
- **Conditions/limitations:** No treatments or doses are given. Whether herpes can be cured is not stated in the pages read, so the answer does not say.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Sores plus fever, or large painful swellings in the groin, need a visit soon.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S16
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** I have itching in my pubic hair or private area. Could it be lice or scabies?
- **Answer:** It could be. Pubic lice are tiny insects that live in pubic hair and usually pass on through sexual contact. A health worker can see the lice and their eggs on the hair. Scabies is a skin condition caused by tiny mites that makes the skin very itchy. In adults it is often caught through sex, but this is not always so. Itching can also have other causes, such as thrush. Both lice and scabies can be treated, so see a health worker rather than guessing.
- **Evidence/claim:** "Pediculosis pubis is caused by the parasite Phthirus pubis and is usually transmitted by sexual contact" / "The clinical diagnosis is based on typical symptoms of itching in the pubic region, lice and nits can be observed on pubic hair." / "Scabies is a skin infestation caused by the mite Sarcoptes scabiei, which causes pruritus." / "Scabies among adults is frequently sexually acquired, although scabies among children usually is not."
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 3.10.1 Pediculosis Pubis, p. 184; s. 3.10.2 Scabies, p. 185
- **Conditions/limitations:** The guideline lists treatments (creams, lotions, tablets); none are named. "Thrush" and other causes of itching come from IG2026 s. 3.5.6 (p. 173), where candidiasis is described as "not an STI" (see NEW-S-08). Do not tell a girl that scabies means she has had sex: among children it usually is not sexually acquired.
- **Safety/referral notes:** If itching is with sores, unusual discharge or pain, she should see a health worker. If she says she was forced, route to the existing help reply (not a topic for this entry).
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S17
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** Can I catch hepatitis B from sex, and how can I prevent it?
- **Answer:** Hepatitis B is a virus that harms the liver. Kenya's 2026 guideline says it spreads through contact with infected blood or other body fluids, mainly through sex, sharing needles, needle-stick injuries, and from an infected mother to her baby at birth. Vaccination is the main way to prevent it, along with safe healthcare practices. Signs can include yellow skin or eyes, dark urine and tiredness. Ask a health worker whether you are vaccinated and whether you should be tested.
- **Evidence/claim:** "Hepatitis B is transmitted horizontally through exposures to infected blood or other body fluids, primarily via sexual contact, sharing needles, occupational exposure (like needle stick injuries), and vertically from an infected mother to her infant during birth." / Prevention: "Hepatitis B birth dose and routine immunization" (Table 4.2). / Features: "Yellowing of the skin and eyes (jaundice)", "Dark-coloured urine", fatigue. / FP7 p. 110: contraceptives "Do not protect against STIs, including hepatitis B and HIV. Individuals at risk should use condoms".
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 4.6.2 p. 192; s. 4.4 Table 4.2 and s. 4.5 p. 189; Kenya MoH, National Family Planning Guideline, 7th ed. (2025), p. 110
- **Conditions/limitations:** The pages read do not give the vaccine schedule or catch-up rules for adolescents, so none is stated. IG2026 p. 57 says the hepatitis B vaccine is for "newborns and high-risk groups" (in a section on people living with HIV); whether a teenager is eligible needs a clinician. Hepatitis A, C and E are not covered.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Yellow skin or eyes, dark urine or stomach pain need a health worker soon. She should not assume she is protected.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S18
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** Could a painless sore or a rash on my palms be syphilis?
- **Answer:** It could. Kenya's 2026 guideline says early syphilis causes a painless sore, usually at the place where the infection got in (the genitals, anus or mouth), about 3 weeks after exposure. Because it does not hurt, it can be missed. Weeks later, about 6 to 8 weeks after the sore, it can cause a skin rash, especially on the palms of the hands and soles of the feet. Syphilis can be found with a blood test and cured with antibiotics, so see a health worker rather than waiting for it to go away.
- **Evidence/claim:** Primary syphilis (table cells, read as fragments): "Painless chancre at" / "site of inoculation" (genital, anal, or oral) / "~3 weeks after" exposure. Secondary syphilis: "Skin rash, especially" / "on palms and soles" / "~6-8 weeks after". Diagnosis by serological testing (RPR/VDRL, then a treponemal test). WHO: syphilis is curable.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 3.4 Table 3.8, p. 166 (primary) and p. 167 (secondary); WHO, Sexually transmitted infections fact sheet
- **Conditions/limitations:** Treatment drugs are not named. The guideline's blood-test details are not explained. Other conditions cause sores (herpes, yeast, injury; s. 3.4 p. 164, see NEW-S-13), so a sore does not by itself mean syphilis. "Curable" rests on WHO, as in NEW-S-02.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Syphilis in pregnancy affects the baby (not covered here); a pregnant girl with a sore should see a health worker without delay.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S19
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** Can I take medicine after unprotected sex to stop HIV?
- **Answer:** Yes. A medicine called PEP can prevent HIV after a possible exposure, but it must be started as soon as possible and no later than 72 hours (3 days) after. It is taken for 28 days. Go to a health facility straight away: you will be offered an HIV test and PEP if it is right for you, and you will also be checked for other STIs and hepatitis B. If the sex was forced, the same facility can give STI treatment and emergency contraception.
- **Evidence/claim:** PEP must be started within 72 hours of exposure; sexual contact is an eligible exposure; the course is 28 days; PEP provision includes hepatitis B screening and STI evaluation; sexual assault care includes STI treatment and emergency contraception.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 1.2 p. 13; Table 1.8 p. 14; PEP management table p. 15; sexual assault care p. 16
- **Conditions/limitations:** Medicine names and doses are deliberately not given. The 72-hour limit is firm in the guideline. The consent rule for adolescents is "in line with national consent guidance" (Table 1.12, p. 17).
- **Safety/referral notes:** Urgent: the time limit is the point. Overlaps with the Safety Net and the held-topic rules (emergency contraception timing, sexual assault). Do not add this entry until the clinicians have approved it and the held-topic rules have been reviewed against it.
- **Status:** pending

## KB-S20
- **Intent:** STIs and HIV
- **User question:** If I have an STI, does my partner need treatment too?
- **Answer:** Yes. Kenya's guidelines say that people who are diagnosed with an STI should be encouraged to tell their sexual partners, and that partners should receive testing and treatment so the infection is not passed back and forth. A health worker can help, for example by notifying your partner for you or giving you a referral.
- **Evidence/claim:** "Implement strategies to encourage individuals diagnosed with STIs to" [...] "inform their sexual partners." [...] "diagnosed individuals receive appropriate testing and treatment to reduce" [...] "the risk of re-infection and further transmission" [...] "Encourage and assist with partner notification through patient referral,"
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), STI prevention approaches table, p. 157; STI case management steps, p. 162
- **Conditions/limitations:** The guidelines are written for health workers. Whether a partner can be treated without being seen is not explained here.
- **Safety/referral notes:** If she is afraid to tell a partner because of violence, she should talk to a health worker; call 1195, 999 or 112 if in danger.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-S21
- **Intent:** STIs and HIV
- **User question:** I have itching, burning and a thick white discharge. Could it be a yeast infection?
- **Answer:** It could be. Kenya's guidelines describe a yeast infection (vaginal candidiasis) as causing itching or burning of the vulva, soreness, and a thick white or creamy discharge that is often like curds. It is not an STI, but its signs look like some STIs, and the same signs can also be caused by other infections, so it needs to be checked. Wet pads can encourage fungal infections like this, so change pads regularly. See a health worker for a test and treatment.
- **Evidence/claim:** "It is therefore not an STI" [...] "Candidiasis presents with pruritus or a burning sensation of the vulva and vaginal soreness or irritation" [...] "it characteristically is curdy, white or creamy and thick" [...] "Wet pads can cause skin irritation and encourage fungal infections like candidiasis"
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), "Candidiasis", p. 173; Kenya MoH and MoE, Menstrual Hygiene Management in Schools: A Handbook for Teachers, "Potential Health Risks Due to Poor Hygiene During Menstruation", p. 19
- **Conditions/limitations:** The guidelines are written for health workers, and the treatment is not given to the girl here. The answer does not tell her to treat herself.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Lower belly pain, a fever, sores or bleeding after sex needs a visit soon. See NEW-S-01.
- **Status:** approved
