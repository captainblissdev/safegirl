# Knowledge Base — STIs

## KB-S3
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** I might have been exposed to HIV. Is there something I can take to stop it?
- **Answer:** Yes — post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is a course of medication started as soon as possible after exposure. Both Kenyan and WHO guidance agree it should be started within 72 hours, since it becomes less effective the longer you wait.
- **Evidence/claim:** PEP (28-day ARV regimen) must be initiated within 72 hours of exposure per both sources — this is one of the few points of full agreement between MoH and WHO.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH YFS Guidelines (2005), Annex 1, pp. 26–28 and WHO (2018), Ch. 7 §7.3.5/7.3.14, pp. 43, 46
- **Conditions/limitations:** Specific drug names/dosing not established by sources — do not include in the answer.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Must direct to immediate facility-based care — this is time-critical.

## KB-S4
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** I have unusual discharge, itching, burning or lower belly pain. Could it be an STI?
- **Answer:** It could be. Unusual discharge, genital sores or lower belly pain can be signs of an STI, but many STIs cause no symptoms at all, so only a test can tell. A health worker can examine you and give treatment, and Kenya's guidelines say providers must ensure privacy and confidentiality when serving adolescents.
- **Evidence/claim:** Lower abdominal pain, pain during sex, bleeding between periods and fever may indicate pelvic inflammatory disease; most STIs have no symptoms; providers must ensure privacy and confidentiality for adolescents.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH, National Guidelines for Prevention, Management and Control of STIs (2018), s. 4.3 p. 39 and s. 4.5.4 p. 44; WHO, Sexually transmitted infections fact sheet
- **Conditions/limitations:** 2018 is the only Kenyan STI guideline edition found, read through a third-party copy. No treatment or drug advice is given.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Lower belly pain with fever, or pain during sex, needs a clinic visit soon. She should not self-treat.

## KB-S5
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** Can STIs be cured, and can they harm me if I leave them?
- **Answer:** Four common STIs (syphilis, gonorrhoea, chlamydia and trichomoniasis) can be cured with antibiotics. HIV and herpes cannot be cured, but medicine can keep them under control. Some untreated infections can spread and cause pelvic inflammatory disease, so it is better to get tested and treated early.
- **Evidence/claim:** Syphilis, gonorrhoea, chlamydia and trichomoniasis are curable; HIV and herpes are not but can be managed; untreated infection can lead to pelvic inflammatory disease.
- **Source:** WHO, Sexually transmitted infections fact sheet; Kenya MoH, STI guidelines (2018), s. 4.3 p. 39
- **Conditions/limitations:** Being cured does not protect against catching the infection again. Partner treatment and finish-the-course advice are not included because no verbatim source text was seen.
- **Safety/referral notes:** She should go to a clinic for testing and treatment rather than self-treating.

## KB-S6
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** Is the HPV vaccine available to me, and am I old enough?
- **Answer:** Kenya's national HPV vaccine programme targets girls from age 10, and since November 2025 it is given as a single dose. If you are older than that, ask at a government health facility whether you can still get it.
- **Evidence/claim:** Kenya's HPV programme targets 10-year-old girls and moved from a two-dose to a single-dose schedule in November 2025.
- **Source:** WHO AFRO, Kenya transitions to single-dose HPV vaccination schedule (page dated 1 November 2025)
- **Conditions/limitations:** Sources disagree on the target age (WHO AFRO: 10-year-olds; news reports: 10 to 14). Whether it is free, and whether catch-up is offered for older girls, was not stated in any primary source.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Do not tell a girl over 14 she is ineligible. She should ask at a clinic.

## KB-S7
- **Intent:** STIs
- **User question:** Can I take PrEP to prevent HIV? I'm a teenager.
- **Answer:** PrEP is medicine that HIV-negative people take to lower their risk of getting HIV. Kenya's 2022 guidelines say it is for sexually active people aged 15 and over who test HIV-negative, and it is available across all 47 counties. A health worker will test you and check it is right for you. PrEP does not protect against other STIs, so condoms still matter.
- **Evidence/claim:** Kenya's 2022 guideline: PrEP should only be offered to clients aged 15 and over who are sexually active and HIV-negative; PrEP is available across the 47 counties.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Kenya HIV Prevention and Treatment Guidelines (2022), s. 11.1.3; Kenya MoH, Framework for the Implementation of PrEP of HIV in Kenya (2022); WHO, HIV fact sheet
- **Conditions/limitations:** A weight criterion (30 kg in the Kenyan guideline, as reported) is not in the answer. Whether 15- to 17-year-olds need parental consent was not found. Eligibility for the dapivirine ring was not found. Check whether Children Act 2022 s. 16(1) affects this.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Girls under 15, or at ongoing risk, should go to a clinic or youth-friendly service. PrEP needs an HIV test first and regular follow-up testing.

### KB-S2 (HIV testing without parents / consent wording)
- **User question:** Can teenagers access HIV testing without their parents?
- **Answer:** Health services for young people are meant to be confidential. The rules on parental consent for under-18s can differ by service, so a health worker can explain what applies to you.
- **Original answer (pre-interim):** WHO guidance recommends that HIV testing services, including HIV self-testing, be accessible to adolescents without mandatory parental or guardian authorization or notification.
- **Evidence:** HIV testing services must be accessible to adolescents without mandatory parental authorization.
- **Source:** WHO Recommendations (2018), Ch. 7 §7.4, pp. 47–48
- **Conditions/limitations:** Partner notification (if relevant) is handled separately with IPV-risk sensitivity — not covered in this entry. Consent wording is interim pending legal review (Children Act 2022 s.16(1)).
- **Status:** 🟡 HOLD. Held: the whole answer was a consent claim; needs a rewrite after legal review.
