# Knowledge Base — General

## KB-G1
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** Will the clinic tell my parents what I talked to them about?
- **Answer:** Youth-friendly health services are expected to keep what you share confidential, including from parents — except in specific situations where the law requires disclosure, which staff should explain to you clearly.
- **Evidence/claim:** Providers must maintain confidentiality and not leak information to parents; legal exceptions exist but aren't specified in this source.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH YFS Guidelines (2005), Ch. 3 §3.2–3.3, pp. 16–17
- **Conditions/limitations:** Exact legal exceptions not established by this source — flag if a user's question implies a specific legal scenario.
- **Safety/referral notes:** None required.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-G2
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** What age counts as "youth" or "adolescent" for these services?
- **Answer:** Kenyan services define adolescents as 10–19, youth as 15–24, and young people as 10–24 — these terms are often used interchangeably.
- **Evidence/claim:** Age range definitions for target populations in Kenya.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH YFS Guidelines (2005), Ch. 1 §1.1.2, p. 2
- **Conditions/limitations:** None significant.
- **Safety/referral notes:** None required.
- **Status:** approved

## KB-G3
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** Is my period normal, and when should I see a health worker?
- **Answer:** Periods vary from girl to girl. Kenya's school handbook says some girls have cycles of 21 days and others 35 days, and bleeding usually lasts 2 to 7 days, with about 5 days the average. In the first years after periods start they may not be regular. WHO says bleeding that is unpredictable, infrequent, very painful or heavy, or that stops you doing normal activities, could point to an underlying health condition, so see a health worker. A late period can have many causes, including pregnancy.
- **Evidence/claim:** MHM: "Some girls menstruate every 28 days while others have longer cycles of 35 days or shorter cycles of 21 days." / "Menstruation usually last from 2-7 days with 5 days being the average length of menstrual flow." / "Menstrual cycle for the first few years is not regular for some girls." WHO: menstrual bleeding that is unpredictable, infrequent, very painful, heavy, or disrupts normal activities could suggest an underlying health condition.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH and MoE, Menstrual Hygiene Management in Schools: A Handbook for Teachers, "Facts about Menstruation", p. 10; WHO, Menstrual health fact sheet
- **Conditions/limitations:** The handbook does not say when bleeding is too heavy or too long, or how many days late a period must be before testing. Confirm the handbook's edition and date (cover says 2020; file name says April 2022).
- **Safety/referral notes:** Very heavy bleeding, or pain that stops usual activities, needs a facility visit. A late period should be routed to a health worker or a pregnancy test, not diagnosed (held-topic group C).
- **Status:** pending

## KB-G4
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** What can I do about period pain?
- **Answer:** Many girls have period pain: WHO says more than 2 in 3 women and girls do. Kenya's school handbook suggests putting a bottle of hot water on your tummy, doing some exercise such as skipping or swimming, drinking plenty of water and eating a healthy, balanced diet. If you want a painkiller, ask a pharmacist or health worker which one is safe for you. See a health worker if the pain goes on for a long time or stops your usual activities.
- **Evidence/claim:** MHM: "Place hot water in a bottle on the abdomen to relieve cramps." / "Physical exercises such as swimming, running, jogging, skipping, hula hoop." / "Eat healthy balanced diet all the time." / "Drink plenty of water." / "Please visit a doctor if you are experiencing discomforts for a longer period of time." WHO: more than 2 out of 3 women and girls have pain with menstrual bleeding. NHS: see a GP if pain stops usual daily activities.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH and MoE, MHM Handbook for Teachers, "Pain Management of Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS)", p. 20; WHO, Menstrual health fact sheet; NHS, Period pain (UK)
- **Conditions/limitations:** The handbook gives no painkiller advice; the painkiller line rests on the NHS source from the 6 Oct draft. No doses. Some painkillers are unsuitable for some people (for example with stomach ulcers or asthma), which was not verified here.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Severe or worsening pelvic pain needs a facility visit. Pain with a late period could be pregnancy-related, so it should not be called just a period.
- **Status:** pending

## KB-G5
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** How do I manage my period hygienically?
- **Answer:** Kenya's school handbook gives these tips: change your pad or other menstrual product regularly, wash your hands with soap and clean water before and after changing it, bathe or shower at least once a day, and change your underwear every day or when it is soiled. Wash the outside of the vagina with water only, and do not put soap, deodorant or liquids inside the vagina. Wipe from front to back after using the toilet.
- **Evidence/claim:** "Taking a bath or shower at least once a day." / "Using of a clean undergarment and changing them regularly, a minimum of every day or when soiled." / "Changing of menstrual product and material regularly during menstruation." / "Ensuring hand washing with soap and clean water before and after changing the product." / "Washing the vagina with water only! … the vagina has its own self-cleaning mechanism. An external cleaning agent or deodorant should not be used and too much soap can cause itching." / "Wiping from front to back following urination or defecation to avoid infections." / "Avoiding douching…" / "Infrequent change of menstrual products can cause urinary tract infections (UTIs) and reproductive tract infections (RTIs)."
- **Source:** Kenya MoH and MoE, MHM Handbook for Teachers, "Safe and Hygiene Practices During Menstruation", p. 21; pp. 19 to 20; Kenya MoH, Menstrual Hygiene Management Policy 2019 to 2030
- **Conditions/limitations:** The handbook gives no number of hours between changes. Tampon and cup advice, including toxic shock syndrome (pp. 19 to 20), is not included without a clinician.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Unusual discharge, itching or burning should be seen at a clinic. Toxic shock signs (sudden high fever, rash, vomiting, confusion) are an emergency but the source puts them under tampon use, so they are held out of this entry.
- **Status:** pending

## KB-G6
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** What changes happen to my body at puberty, and when does my first period come?
- **Answer:** The first sign of puberty in most girls is breast 'buds', followed by body hair and a growth spurt. Kenya's school handbook says a girl's first period happens between the ages of 9 and 17, and some girls get theirs as early as 9. Periods may be irregular for the first few years. Timing varies from girl to girl, so if you are worried about your development, talk to a health worker.
- **Evidence/claim:** AAP: breast buds are the earliest sign of puberty. MHM: the first period occurs "between 9-17 years"; "Some girls have their first menstruation as early as nine years of age."; "Menstrual cycle for the first few years is not regular for some girls."
- **Source:** Kenya MoH and MoE, MHM Handbook for Teachers, "Menstruation and Semenarche" p. 8 and "Facts about Menstruation" p. 10; American Academy of Pediatrics, HealthyChildren.org, Physical Development in Girls (2019)
- **Conditions/limitations:** The handbook's own diagram on p. 8 says an egg matures "between the ages of 8 and 19 years"; the 9 to 17 range is used. The AAP source is American. KDHS 2022 was not opened.
- **Safety/referral notes:** If there are no signs of puberty, or no period well past the usual ages, talk to a health worker. No verified age thresholds for worry were found.
- **Status:** pending

## KB-G7
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** What does a youth-friendly health service offer, and what should I expect?
- **Answer:** Kenya's 2025 family planning guideline says services for adolescents and youth should be equitable, accessible, acceptable, appropriate and effective. In practice that means services should be affordable, open at convenient times such as after school and on weekends, give you information you can understand, and keep what you say confidential, including when you register and in your records. You should be able to speak to a provider in a place where no one can see or hear you.
- **Evidence/claim:** "Healthcare providers … shall provide adolescent and youth-friendly services that are equitable, accessible, acceptable, appropriate and effective…" / "SRH services must be affordable and available to all adolescents and youth." / "…made obtainable during convenient hours, including after school or work and during weekends and holidays…" / "Confidentiality should be maintained at all times, including during registration, consultation, record-keeping and disclosure." / "The point of service delivery should be located in a place that affords auditory and visual privacy." / "The services should offer appropriate information that will be understood by the client…"
- **Source:** Kenya MoH, National Family Planning Guideline, 7th ed. (2025), "FP Services for Adolescents and Youth", p. 23; Table 3.3 "Principles for the provision of adolescent and youth-responsive services", p. 24
- **Conditions/limitations:** These are standards for how services should be delivered; a given facility may not meet them. The pages describe family planning services; they do not list a full package of services, facility types or helplines, so none is named. The 2005 and 2016 YFS guidelines are not used. The same section (p. 25) says adolescents under 18 give assent and a guardian gives consent for FP services; this entry does not state a consent rule.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Urgent symptoms (heavy bleeding, severe pain, possible pregnancy) need prompt care.
- **Status:** pending

## KB-G8
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** Why do I feel tired, moody or get pimples before my period?
- **Answer:** That could be premenstrual syndrome (PMS), a group of symptoms linked to your cycle that start 1 to 2 weeks before your period. Kenya's school handbook lists tender breasts, pimples, tiredness, trouble sleeping, headache or backache, bloating, changes in appetite, and mood swings or feeling tearful. For some girls PMS is just a monthly bother, and for others it is so strong that it is hard to get through the day. It goes away when your periods stop, for example during pregnancy. If it is making daily life hard, or you feel very sad or anxious, see a health worker.
- **Evidence/claim:** "PMS symptoms occur 1 to 2 weeks before your menstruation or monthly bleeding starts." / "For some people, PMS is just a monthly bother. For others, it may be so severe that it makes it hard to even get through the day." / Symptoms listed: "Acne/pimples", "Swollen or tender breasts", "Feeling tired", "Trouble sleeping", "Headache or backache", "Tension, irritability, mood swings, or crying spells", "Anxiety or depression". / "PMS goes away when your monthly periods stop, such as when you get pregnant or go through menopause."
- **Source:** Kenya MoH and MoE, MHM in Schools: A Handbook for Teachers, "Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS)", p. 9; "Pain Management of Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS)", p. 20
- **Conditions/limitations:** The handbook does not say what counts as severe or when to see a health worker for PMS (the "visit a doctor" tip on p. 20 is about long-lasting discomfort). Late-period and pregnancy questions go to the held-topic rules. See NEW-G-02 for cramps.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Persistent low mood or anxiety that does not go away needs a health worker or counsellor. If she says she wants to harm herself, the Safety Net reply applies, not this entry.
- **Status:** pending

## KB-G9
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** What can I use during my period: pads, tampons or a cup?
- **Answer:** Kenya's school handbook lists four choices: disposable pads, reusable pads, tampons and menstrual cups. The choice depends on what you prefer, whether you have water and a place to dispose of products, and what suits your underwear. Disposable pads are easy to use but cost more and are not always available. Reusable pads cost less but need water and somewhere private to wash and dry them; if they are not cleaned well they can cause infections, and you should not share them. Tampons and cups go inside the vagina, and need instructions before you use them. A tampon left in too long can cause a serious illness called toxic shock syndrome, so change it often. Keep products in a clean, cool, dry place.
- **Evidence/claim:** "Existing products and materials include disposable and reusable sanitary pads, tampons, and reusable menstrual cups." / "The choice between reusable and disposable products depends predominantly on:" "One's choice", "Availability of disposal and water facilities", "The compatibility with girl's and women's preferred undergarments." / Disposable pads: "Not easily available in rural areas", "Relatively costly". / Reusable pads: "Requires access to water", "for washing the pad", "Need for somewhere" / "private to wash and dry", "If not hygienically" / "cleaned it can predispose" / "including candidasis", "not to share". / Tampons: "Requires instruction before use"; "If hygiene and frequent changing of tampons is not maintained can lead to Toxic Shock Syndrome (TSS)". / Cups: "Requires instructions before use". / "Menstrual products and materials should be stored in a clean, cool and dry place that is free from dust, smoke and chemicals"
- **Source:** Kenya MoH and MoE, MHM in Schools: A Handbook for Teachers, "Menstrual Products", pp. 14 to 16 (choice p. 14; storage tip and table pp. 15 to 16)
- **Conditions/limitations:** The handbook says some cultures and religions do not accept tampons or cups; not included. It gives no number of hours between changes (see NEW-G-03). It is a teacher's handbook, not clinical guidance. Toxic shock signs are not listed in the pages used.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Sudden high fever, rash, vomiting or feeling very unwell while using a tampon is an emergency (not stated in the pages read; the clinician should confirm adding this).
- **Status:** pending

## KB-G10
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** How do I dispose of a used pad safely?
- **Answer:** Kenya's school handbook says to wrap the used pad in paper, put it in a dustbin and close the lid. Keep period waste separate from other rubbish by using a sanitary bin where there is one. Throwing pads in pit latrines or toilets is shown as the wrong way, because it blocks drains and can pollute water.
- **Evidence/claim:** Figure 1, "Proper storage of menstrual waste": "Wrap in paper", "Put in dustbin", "Close the lid". / "Girls and women should separate menstrual waste from the general waste by explicitly disposing of the menstrual waste in designated sanitary bins." / "In the absence of bins, awareness should be raised on how to safely and hygienically handle used disposable sanitary pads." / The handbook's "Improper Ways of Disposing Menstrual Waste" figure shows "Pit latrines" (with pour-flush and western toilets). / Harms of improper disposal include "Clogging of drainage system.", water contamination, and "Scavenging poses the risk of contracting diseases from infected menstrual waste."
- **Source:** Kenya MoH and MoE, MHM in Schools: A Handbook for Teachers, "Safe Disposal of Used Menstrual Products", pp. 24 to 25
- **Conditions/limitations:** The last sentence ("keep it until you can dispose of it safely") is not stated in the handbook; the clinician or Mr. Ouma should keep it or remove it. Incineration and community collection are not included. The "improper ways" labels come from a figure caption, so confirm them against the printed page.
- **Safety/referral notes:** None beyond hygiene; wash hands after changing (see NEW-G-03).
- **Status:** pending

## KB-G11
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** What is a period, and why does it happen?
- **Answer:** A period is a regular discharge of blood and tissue from the lining of the womb (uterus), which comes out through the vagina. Kenya's school handbook explains it like this: usually one egg matures in each cycle and travels from the ovary down the fallopian tube, and meanwhile the womb lining gets thicker. If the egg is not fertilised, the body sheds the lining as blood, and that is your period. If the egg is fertilised, the lining provides a home for a growing baby. A period is a normal part of growing up. It starts at puberty and stops later in life at menopause.
- **Evidence/claim:** "Menstruation is a regular discharge of blood and tissue from inner lining of the uterus through the vagina." / "Typically, one egg matures every cycle." / "If there is no fertilization, the body sheds the uterus lining in the form of blood or blood clots" / "If the egg is fertilized, this lining of the uterus provides a home to a growing baby" / "menstruation is a normal biological process and it happens to girls when they get to puberty and ends at menopause"
- **Source:** Kenya MoH and MoE, MHM in Schools: A Handbook for Teachers, "Menstruation Cycle", p. 8; "Psychological Well Being", p. 21
- **Conditions/limitations:** Explains the cycle only; it does not say when a girl can get pregnant, which the handbook does not state. The 28-day cycle is not stated (see NEW-G-01). Pregnancy chances (during a period, first time) need another source and clinician input.
- **Safety/referral notes:** None beyond NEW-G-01 (when to see a health worker).
- **Status:** pending
