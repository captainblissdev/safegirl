# Knowledge Base — General

## KB-G1
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** Will the clinic tell my parents what I talked to them about?
- **Answer:** Youth-friendly health services are expected to keep what you share confidential, including from parents — except in specific situations where the law requires disclosure, which staff should explain to you clearly.
- **Evidence/claim:** Providers must maintain confidentiality and not leak information to parents; legal exceptions exist but aren't specified in this source.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH YFS Guidelines (2005), Ch. 3 §3.2–3.3, pp. 16–17
- **Conditions/limitations:** Exact legal exceptions not established by this source — flag if a user's question implies a specific legal scenario.
- **Safety/referral notes:** None required.

## KB-G2
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** What age counts as "youth" or "adolescent" for these services?
- **Answer:** Kenyan services define adolescents as 10–19, youth as 15–24, and young people as 10–24 — these terms are often used interchangeably.
- **Evidence/claim:** Age range definitions for target populations in Kenya.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH YFS Guidelines (2005), Ch. 1 §1.1.2, p. 2
- **Conditions/limitations:** None significant.
- **Safety/referral notes:** None required.

## KB-G3
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** Is my period normal, and when should I see a health worker?
- **Answer:** In the first years after periods start they are often irregular. WHO says bleeding that is unpredictable, infrequent, very painful or heavy, or that stops you doing normal activities, could point to an underlying health condition, so see a health worker. A late period can have many causes, including pregnancy.
- **Evidence/claim:** WHO: menstrual bleeding that is unpredictable, infrequent, very painful, heavy, or disrupts normal activities could suggest an underlying health condition. NHS: periods are more likely to be irregular when they first start in puberty.
- **Source:** WHO, Menstrual health fact sheet; NHS, Irregular periods (UK)
- **Conditions/limitations:** No numeric cycle-length range is given: the figures found (an NHS adult definition of under 21 or over 35 days) are not adolescent- or Kenya-specific, and no Kenyan source gave a range.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Very heavy bleeding, or pain that stops usual activities, needs a facility visit. A late period should be routed to a health worker or a pregnancy test, not diagnosed.

## KB-G4
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** What can I do about period pain?
- **Answer:** Many girls have period pain: WHO says more than 2 in 3 women and girls do. A warm bath or shower, or a warm heat pad on your tummy, can help. Ask a pharmacist or health worker which painkiller and dose is safe for you. See a health worker if the pain stops your usual activities, or is new or worse than usual.
- **Evidence/claim:** WHO: more than 2 out of 3 women and girls have pain with menstrual bleeding. NHS: warm bath or shower and a heat pad can help; see a GP if pain stops usual daily activities.
- **Source:** WHO, Menstrual health fact sheet; NHS, Period pain (UK)
- **Conditions/limitations:** The self-care advice comes from a UK source; no Kenyan source on period pain was found. No doses are given. Some painkillers are unsuitable for some people (for example with stomach ulcers or asthma), which was not verified in these sources.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Severe or worsening pelvic pain needs a facility visit. Pain with a late period could be pregnancy-related, so it should not be called just a period.

## KB-G5
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** How do I manage my period hygienically?
- **Answer:** Kenya's national policy aims for every girl to manage her period hygienically, with dignity and without stigma. It says girls should learn which safe menstrual products to use, how to use and care for them, and how to dispose of them. Practical tips such as how often to change a pad are not covered here, so a nurse, teacher or pharmacist can advise.
- **Evidence/claim:** Kenya's policy mission: all women and girls can manage menstruation hygienically, freely, with dignity and without stigma; Objective 3 covers knowledge of safe products, proper use and care, and disposal.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH, Menstrual Hygiene Management Policy 2019-2030 (copy hosted by wesnetwork.org), p. vi and p. 13
- **Conditions/limitations:** The policy is high level and gives no practical steps (how often to change, washing, maximum wear time). A third-party copy was read, so check it against the MoH original.
- **Safety/referral notes:** None specific.

## KB-G6
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** What changes happen to my body at puberty, and when does my first period come?
- **Answer:** The first sign of puberty in most girls is breast 'buds', followed by body hair and a growth spurt. Most girls get their first period within 2 to 3 years after breast development starts. Timing varies from girl to girl, so if you are worried about your development, talk to a health worker.
- **Evidence/claim:** AAP: breast buds are the earliest sign of puberty; most girls get their first period within 2 to 3 years after breast buds develop.
- **Source:** American Academy of Pediatrics, HealthyChildren.org, Physical Development in Girls: What to Expect During Puberty (2019)
- **Conditions/limitations:** The source is American. Its average first-period age (about 12) differs from the median in Kenya's MHM policy (14.4 years, reported as a paraphrase), so no ages are given. KDHS 2022 was not opened.
- **Safety/referral notes:** If there are no signs of puberty, or no period well past typical ages, talk to a health worker. No verified age thresholds were found.

## KB-G7
- **Intent:** General
- **User question:** What does a youth-friendly health service offer, and what should I expect?
- **Answer:** Kenya's national guidelines say youth-friendly services should be accessible, acceptable, appropriate, effective and equitable: free or affordable, with providers who do not judge you. They should offer counselling on growth and development, relationships and sexuality, and services such as contraception counselling, pregnancy testing, and STI and HIV care. These can be found at health facilities, pharmacies, mobile clinics and help lines.
- **Evidence/claim:** Youth-friendly services are defined as accessible, acceptable, appropriate, effective and equitable; the package includes counselling on growth and development, relationships and sexuality; delivery points include facilities, pharmacies, mobile clinics and help lines.
- **Source:** Kenya MoH, National Guidelines for Provision of Adolescent and Youth Friendly Services in Kenya (2016), pp. 8, 18-19, 28-29, 34; Kenya MoH, National Adolescent Sexual and Reproductive Health Policy (2015), s. 6.2.3.6, p. 24
- **Conditions/limitations:** Written as what services 'should' offer: practice varies and a given facility may not offer everything. No verified facility directory, helpline or hours were found. The 2016 edition may have been superseded. Post-abortion care and sexual violence content in the guidelines is held and not used here.
- **Safety/referral notes:** Urgent symptoms (heavy bleeding, severe pain, possible pregnancy) need prompt care.
