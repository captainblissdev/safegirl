# Contraception

## 1. NEW-C-01

**Intent:** Contraception

**User question:** Which birth control methods can a teenager use?

**Answer:** Kenya's 2025 family planning guideline says adolescents and youth can safely use any contraceptive method that follows the guideline and the medical eligibility criteria. A health worker will help you choose and will explain the possible side effects, because teenagers may find side effects harder to put up with. Using condoms as well gives protection from STIs and HIV.

**Evidence/claim:** "Adolescents and youth in need of contraceptive services can safely use any method that follows the guideline and MEC criteria." / "Adolescents may be less tolerant of side effects. Healthcare providers shall explain the possible side effects during counselling…" / "Adolescents and youth shall be sensitized on the use of condoms for dual protection against pregnancy and STI/HIV."

**Source:** Kenya MoH, National Family Planning Guideline, 7th ed. (2025), "FP Services for Adolescents and Youth", p. 25; WHO, Family Planning: A Global Handbook for Providers (2022), Ch. 1, p. 5

**Conditions/limitations:** Medical conditions can change which method suits a girl. No consent rule is stated here (see open issue).

**Safety/referral notes:** A health worker should confirm the choice, especially if she has a medical condition or might already be pregnant.

## 2. NEW-C-02

**Intent:** Contraception

**User question:** Do I still need a condom if I'm on the pill or the implant?

**Answer:** Yes, if you want protection from STIs. WHO says condoms are the only contraceptive method that prevents both pregnancy and STIs, including HIV, and Kenya's 2025 guideline says condoms offer dual protection. Other methods prevent pregnancy only, so young people are encouraged to use condoms as well.

**Evidence/claim:** "Male and female condoms help prevent both pregnancy and most STIs (including HIV)…" / "Therefore, condoms offer dual protection against unintended pregnancies and sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including HIV." / "Adolescents and youth shall be sensitized on the use of condoms for dual protection against pregnancy and STI/HIV." WHO: condoms are the only method that prevents both pregnancy and STIs.

**Source:** Kenya MoH, National Family Planning Guideline, 7th ed. (2025), "Male condom", p. 173 and p. 25; WHO, Family planning/contraception methods fact sheet (3 July 2025)

**Conditions/limitations:** Protection depends on using condoms every time and correctly. Step-by-step use is not included (needs the full table on pp. 176 to 178 and a clinician).

**Safety/referral notes:** If a condom breaks or slips, or she has had unprotected sex, she should see a health worker promptly (see NEW-C-06, on hold). She should also go for any STI symptoms.

## 3. NEW-C-03

**Intent:** Contraception

**User question:** Will contraception make me infertile or stop me having babies later?

**Answer:** No. WHO states that modern contraceptive methods do not cause infertility. Kenya's 2025 guideline says fertility comes back fairly quickly after you stop: on average it can take 3 to 6 months for most methods, and sometimes up to 12 months after the injection. If it takes much longer, a health worker can look for other causes.

**Evidence/claim:** WHO: modern contraceptive methods do not cause infertility. "After stopping a contraceptive, a woman may expect an average of 3 to 6 months delay in returning to fertility for most methods and sometimes up to 12 months for DMPA." / "In cases of prolonged delay in the return to fertility, underlying causes of female sub-fertility or infertility may need to be considered…"

**Source:** Kenya MoH, National Family Planning Guideline, 7th ed. (2025), "Return to fertility after contraception", p. 31; WHO, Family planning/contraception methods fact sheet (2025); WHO, Family Planning Handbook (2022), Ch. 1, p. 3

**Conditions/limitations:** The same guideline says return is "immediate" for progestin-only pills (p. 74) and the IUCD (p. 134) and gives other figures for injectables (pp. 96, 108), so no per-method figures are given. Does not cover infertility caused by untreated STIs.

**Safety/referral notes:** If she is worried about her fertility, or has pelvic pain or unusual discharge, she should see a health worker.

## 4. NEW-C-07

**Intent:** Contraception

**User question:** Is irregular bleeding normal with the implant or the injection?

**Answer:** Light spotting or irregular bleeding is common with both. The guideline says it is common with the injection, especially in the first 6 to 8 months, and with the implant, especially in the first year. It says this is not harmful and usually does not need treatment. If the bleeding keeps going or worries you, see a health worker.

**Evidence/claim:** Injectables: "Spotting or light bleeding is common during use of injectable contraceptives, particularly during the first 6-8 months of use. It is not harmful. Reassure the client." / "If the bleeding is persistent, assess for gynaecological problems and treat accordingly". Implant: "Reassure client that light bleeding/spotting is common in women using this method especially in the first year. It is not serious and usually does not require treatment." / "If the bleeding is persistent assess for gynaecological problems and treat accordingly".

**Source:** Kenya MoH, National Family Planning Guideline, 7th ed. (2025), Table 4.14 (injectables) p. 105; Table 4.20 (implants) p. 117

**Conditions/limitations:** Drug treatments listed in the tables are not included. Heavy bleeding is not covered by these rows.

**Safety/referral notes:** Persistent or heavy bleeding, pain, or a late period with a possible pregnancy need a health worker.

## 5. NEW-C-08

**Intent:** Contraception

**User question:** How does the contraceptive implant work, and how long does it last?

**Answer:** The implant is one or two small rods that a trained health worker places under the skin of your upper arm. It slowly releases a hormone that prevents pregnancy. Kenya's 2025 guideline says it is 99.9% effective and starts working 24 hours after it is put in. Depending on the type, it lasts 3 or 5 years. Changes in your bleeding are common, such as spotting or longer or missing periods. It does not protect against STIs, so use condoms too if you are at risk. Only a trained health worker can put it in and take it out.

**Evidence/claim:** "Implants provide 99.9% effective protection against pregnancy. They are effective 24 hours post insertion." / Table 4.17: Jadelle, 2 rods, 5 years; Implanon and Implanon NXT, 1 rod, 3 years; Levoplant, 2 rods, 3 years. / "The client cannot initiate or discontinue the method on her own as it requires a trained healthcare provider to insert and remove the implant" / "Insertion and removal require minor surgical procedures and may be uncomfortable" / "Change in menstrual pattern including; amenorrhoea, spotting, intermenstrual bleeding or prolonged bleeding." / "Do not protect against STIs, including hepatitis B and HIV. Individuals at risk should use condoms in addition to the implants."

**Source:** Kenya MoH, National Family Planning Guideline, 7th ed. (2025), "Contraceptive implants", pp. 109 to 110 (Table 4.17 on p. 109)

**Conditions/limitations:** Brand names are left out. Some medicines (some HIV medicines, for example) can make implants work less well (p. 109 key messages); not included. Side effects such as headache, nausea and mood changes are not listed. Fertility after removal is not stated because the guideline contradicts itself (see conflicts above).

**Safety/referral notes:** A health worker must confirm that the implant suits her and that she is not pregnant. Pain, redness or swelling at the arm, or very heavy bleeding, needs a clinic visit (not stated in the pages read; ask the clinician to confirm this line or remove it).

## 6. NEW-C-09

**Intent:** Contraception

**User question:** How does the contraceptive injection work, and how often do I need it?

**Answer:** The injection is given by a health worker. The most common kind is given every three months (13 weeks); another kind is given every two months. Kenya's 2025 guideline says it is 99% effective if you get each injection on time, and that the risk of pregnancy is highest when you are late or miss one. Changes in bleeding are common: irregular, heavy or long bleeding, light spotting, or no periods at all. After you stop, it can take longer to get pregnant than with most other methods: four months or longer, and sometimes up to 12 months. It does not protect against STIs, so use condoms too if you are at risk.

**Evidence/claim:** "Given every three months (13 weeks)" (DMPA) and "Given every two months" (NET-EN), Table 4.11. / "Effectiveness depends on receiving injections on time. Risk of pregnancy is greatest when a woman is late for or misses an injection." / "POIC is 99% effective if used correctly and consistently" / Side effects include "irregular bleeding", "heavy and prolonged bleeding", "light spotting or bleeding", "amenorrhoea, especially after one year of use". / "Return of fertility may be delayed for four months or longer after discontinuation" / "sometimes up to 12 months for DMPA" / "They offer no protection against STIs, including hepatitis B and HIV; individuals at risk for these should use condoms in addition to injectable contraceptives"

**Source:** Kenya MoH, National Family Planning Guideline, 7th ed. (2025), "Injectable contraceptives", p. 94 (Table 4.11); p. 95 (effectiveness); p. 96 (limitations and side effects); p. 32 (return to fertility, up to 12 months for DMPA)

**Conditions/limitations:** No age is stated (see conflict 1 above). The self-injected kind (DMPA-SC) is not covered. The grace period for late injections is not given. Return-to-fertility wording now matches NEW-C-03 and NEW-C-21 (four months or longer, sometimes up to 12 months); the guideline's own figures vary (pp. 31, 96, 108), and the combined injectable is given as about 5 months.

**Safety/referral notes:** If she is late for an injection and has had unprotected sex, she should see a health worker soon (a held-topic area; do not build more detail without a clinician).

## 7. NEW-C-10

**Intent:** Contraception

**User question:** How does the combined contraceptive pill work, and what are the side effects?

**Answer:** The combined pill has two hormones and is taken once a day, ideally at the same time every day. Kenya's 2025 guideline says it is 99.7% effective if used correctly and consistently. The risk of pregnancy is highest if you start a new pack 3 or more days late or miss 3 or more pills near the start or end of a pack. Mild side effects such as nausea, spotting between periods, headaches, breast tenderness and mood or weight changes are common, especially in the first three months. Severe vomiting or diarrhoea, and some medicines (for example some TB, epilepsy and HIV medicines), can make it work less well, so tell the health worker. The pill does not protect against STIs. If you miss pills, ask a health worker what to do, because it depends on how many you missed and when.

**Evidence/claim:** "COCs are 99.7% effective in preventing pregnancy if used correctly and consistently." / "The risk of pregnancy is greatest when a woman starts a new pill pack 3 or more days late, or misses 3 or more pills near the beginning or the end of a pill pack." / "COCs must be taken daily to be effective, preferably at the same time each day." / "Contraceptive effectiveness could also be lowered in the presence of gastroenteritis, severe vomiting, and diarrhoea." / "Effectiveness may be lowered if the client is on anti-TB drugs" / "Nausea (more common in the first three months)" / "COCs do not offer protection against STls, including hepatitis B and HIV." / MEC Category 1: "Age: from menarche to 40 years"

**Source:** Kenya MoH, National Family Planning Guideline, 7th ed. (2025), "Combined Oral Contraceptive (COCs) Pills", p. 58 (what it is); p. 59 (effectiveness, first limitations); p. 60 (limitations, side effects); p. 61 (eligibility)

**Conditions/limitations:** Missed-pill instructions are conditional tables (Table 4.4, p. 72) and are not summarised here (see appendix to `kb-candidates-2025-fp-guideline.md`). Serious but rare side effects (blood clots, stroke) are listed on p. 60 and are not included without a clinician. The pill does not suit everyone (medical conditions change eligibility).

**Safety/referral notes:** A health worker should check that the pill is suitable. Severe chest pain, a very bad headache, or a swollen painful leg need urgent care (not stated in the pages read; the clinician should decide whether to add warning signs).

## 8. NEW-C-06 (held topic: medic approved; serve only once the held-topic rules review is logged)

**Intent:** Contraception

**User question:** What should I do if a condom breaks or slips?

**Answer:** Go to a health worker or pharmacist as soon as you can. Kenya's guideline says that when a condom breaks or semen spills, the health worker should offer emergency contraception and talk to you about HIV and STIs. Emergency contraception works better the sooner it is used and can be used up to 5 days (120 hours) after unprotected sex.

**Evidence/claim:** "NOTE: In case of semen spillage or breakage of condom during use, the healthcare provider should offer emergency contraception and counsel on HIV and STIs." / "Emergency contraception pills should be started as soon as possible but within 120 hours of unprotected sex." / "ECs are 98% effective if used correctly; i.e., taking the ECs within 120 hours. The earlier the EC is used after unprotected sexual intercourse, the more effective it is." / "NOTE: Copper IUCD is now recommended as a method for emergency contraception if inserted within five (5) days of unprotected sex."

**Source:** Kenya MoH, National Family Planning Guideline, 7th ed. (2025), p. 177 (condom breakage); p. 90 (EC within 120 hours); p. 86 (effectiveness); p. 87 (copper IUCD)

**Conditions/limitations:** Methods, doses and "98% effective" are not stated in the answer. Not a pregnancy test. Where HIV exposure is possible, PEP must start within 72 hours (see NEW-S-06).

**Safety/referral notes:** Time-sensitive. This is in the held-topic area ("some options work best soon after sex"); do not build until the clinicians have approved it and the held-topic rules have been reviewed against it.

# STIs and HIV

## 9. NEW-S-01

**Intent:** STIs

**User question:** I have unusual discharge, itching, burning or lower belly pain. Could it be an STI?

**Answer:** It could be, but unusual discharge can have other causes too, and only a test can tell which. Many STIs cause no symptoms at all. Kenya's 2026 guidelines now prefer lab tests that find the exact infection before treatment, so ask for a test at a health facility. Lower belly pain, pain during sex or bleeding between periods needs a visit soon, because untreated infections can spread to the womb and tubes.

**Evidence/claim:** Laboratory-confirmed (aetiological) diagnosis is the preferred approach to STI care in Kenya. Chlamydia symptoms in women include abnormal vaginal discharge, pain on passing urine, and bleeding after sex or between periods; gonorrhoea in women may cause abnormal discharge and lower abdominal discomfort. Untreated infection can ascend to cause pelvic inflammatory disease, ectopic pregnancy and infertility.

**Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 3.3.1 p. 161; s. 3.5.1 Chlamydia p. 171; s. 3.5.2 Gonorrhoea p. 171; WHO, Sexually transmitted infections fact sheet

**Conditions/limitations:** Where a facility has no laboratory, the guideline still allows clinical diagnosis (s. 3.3.2 p. 162). No treatment or drug advice is given. The 2018 guideline (the earlier source) was replaced by the 2026 edition; the privacy sentence that came from it is dropped.

**Safety/referral notes:** Lower belly pain with fever, or pain during sex, needs a clinic visit soon. She should not self-treat.

## 10. NEW-S-05

**Intent:** STIs

**User question:** Can I have an STI without knowing?

**Answer:** Yes. Many STIs cause no symptoms. For example, chlamydia causes no symptoms in about 7 in 10 women, and gonorrhoea is also often symptom-free in women. If they are not treated, they can spread to the womb and tubes and later cause pain, a pregnancy outside the womb, or trouble getting pregnant. The only way to know is a test at a health facility.

**Evidence/claim:** Genital chlamydia infection is asymptomatic in about 70% of women; gonorrhoea is often asymptomatic in women (fewer than half of infected women report symptoms); untreated infections may cause PID, ectopic pregnancy and tubal infertility; laboratory testing also detects asymptomatic infection.

**Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 3.5.1 p. 171; s. 3.5.2 p. 171; s. 3.3.1 p. 161

**Conditions/limitations:** The 70% figure is the guideline's figure for chlamydia in women. It is not given for other STIs. Not every untreated infection causes complications.

**Safety/referral notes:** Anyone who has had sex without a condom, or whose partner may have an STI, can ask for a test at a clinic. She does not have to have symptoms.

## 11. NEW-S-08

**Intent:** STIs

**User question:** Is vaginal discharge always a sign of an STI?

**Answer:** No. Bacterial vaginosis, the most common cause of vaginal discharge in women of childbearing age, is not an STI, and neither is a yeast infection (thrush). Trichomoniasis is an STI that can cause a yellow or greenish, sometimes frothy, discharge and itching, and many women who have it have no symptoms. Because these can look alike, you can't tell by yourself. A health worker can test you and tell you which it is.

**Evidence/claim:** BV is the most common cause of vaginal discharge in women of childbearing age and is not an STI. Candidiasis is not an STI. About 50 to 85% of women with T. vaginalis may have no symptoms; symptomatic women usually have abnormal discharge, and about half have vulval itching, with a yellow or greenish, sometimes frothy discharge.

**Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 3.5.4 p. 172; s. 3.5.5 p. 172; s. 3.5.6 p. 173

**Conditions/limitations:** No description of normal discharge is given because none was found in the pages read. No treatments are given. This overlaps with the "discharge" gap topic, so the gynaecologist should approve it as the answer or replace it.

**Safety/referral notes:** Lower belly pain, fever or pain during sex needs a visit soon. A girl who may be pregnant should be seen before taking any treatment.

## 12. NEW-S-13

**Intent:** STIs

**User question:** I have painful sores or blisters on my genitals. Could it be herpes or syphilis?

**Answer:** It could be. Painful blisters or sores, with itching or burning when you pass urine, can be genital herpes, which can come back. ## 13. NEW-S-11

## 16. NEW-S-16

**Intent:** STIs

## 17. NEW-S-15

## 21. NEW-S-07

**Intent:** STIs

**User question:** If a person with HIV takes their medicine, can they still pass HIV to a partner during sex?

**Answer:** Not if their viral load stays undetectable. Kenya's 2026 guidelines say that people whose blood tests show an undetectable viral load on two tests six months apart have effectively no risk of passing HIV on through sex. This is called U=U: undetectable equals untransmittable. It depends on carrying on with the medicine and the clinic's viral load tests. It does not apply in pregnancy and breastfeeding, to injecting drug use, or to needle-stick injuries.

**Evidence/claim:** Definition: a durable undetectable viral load (two consecutive results below 200 copies/ml six months apart) means effectively no risk of sexual transmission. U=U messaging is not applicable in pregnant and breastfeeding women living with HIV, people who inject drugs, or needle-stick injuries.

**Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 1.6.5 p. 97

**Conditions/limitations:** This is about HIV only; it says nothing about other STIs. A person cannot tell her partner's viral load without the partner's own results.

**Safety/referral notes:** A partner of someone with HIV can ask a clinic about testing and prevention (PrEP or PEP). For other STIs, a test is still the only way to know.

## 23. NEW-S-10

**Intent:** STIs

**User question:** How soon after sex can an HIV test tell me if I have HIV?

**Answer:** Not straight away. After HIV gets into the body there is a "window period" before a test can detect it, and different tests detect it at different times, so an early negative result may not be reliable. A health worker will tell you when to test again. If a result is unclear, Kenya's guidelines say to test again after 14 days. After a possible exposure, check-up tests are repeated at 28 days and at 12 weeks.

**Evidence/claim:** The window period is the time between exposure and detectable antibodies; different tests detect at different stages. Inconclusive results are retested after 14 days. After PEP, HIV testing is repeated at 28 days and 12 weeks; blood or organ donation is avoided until confirmed HIV-negative at 12 weeks.

**Source:** Kenya MoH/NASCOP, Integrated Guidelines (2026), s. 1.1.7 Table 1.6 p. 12; s. 1.2.4 p. 16; s. 1.2.5 p. 16

**Conditions/limitations:** No single number of days is given for when a test becomes reliable. The 28-day and 12-week schedule is the PEP follow-up schedule, not a general rule for all tests.

**Safety/referral notes:** A possible exposure within the last 3 days is urgent: go to a facility about PEP (see NEW-S-06). Use condoms until the result is confirmed.

## 35. NEW-G-09

**Intent:** General

**User question:** What is a period, and why does it happen?

**Answer:** A period is a regular discharge of blood and tissue from the lining of the womb (uterus), which comes out through the vagina. Kenya's school handbook explains it like this: usually one egg matures in each cycle and travels from the ovary down the fallopian tube, and meanwhile the womb lining gets thicker. If the egg is not fertilised, the body sheds the lining as blood, and that is your period. If the egg is fertilised, the lining provides a home for a growing baby. A period is a normal part of growing up. It starts at puberty and stops later in life at menopause.

**Evidence/claim:** "Menstruation is a regular discharge of blood and tissue from inner lining of the uterus through the vagina." / "Typically, one egg matures every cycle." / "If there is no fertilization, the body sheds the uterus lining in the form of blood or blood clots" / "If the egg is fertilized, this lining of the uterus provides a home to a growing baby" / "menstruation is a normal biological process and it happens to girls when they get to puberty and ends at menopause"

**Source:** Kenya MoH and MoE, MHM in Schools: A Handbook for Teachers, "Menstruation Cycle", p. 8; "Psychological Well Being", p. 21

**Conditions/limitations:** Explains the cycle only; it does not say when a girl can get pregnant, which the handbook does not state. The 28-day cycle is not stated (see NEW-G-01). Pregnancy chances (during a period, first time) need another source and clinician input.

## 51. NEW-C-19

**Intent:** Contraception

**User question:** I am breastfeeding. Which contraception can I use?

**Answer:** Breastfeeding can protect against pregnancy for a while, but only if three things are all true. Kenya's family planning guideline calls this the breastfeeding method (LAM, the lactational amenorrhoea method): your periods have not come back, your baby is fed only on breast milk, and your baby is under six months old. Used this way it is up to 98% effective. When any one of the three stops being true, you need another family planning method straight away. A health worker can tell you which methods are safe while you breastfeed.

**Evidence/claim:** "The woman's menstrual periods have not resumed" [...] "The baby is exclusively breastfed" [...] "The baby is less than six months old" [...] "LAM is up to 98% effective if practiced during an exclusive breastfeeding period" [...] "When any of these three criteria is no longer met, another FP method must be introduced in a timely manner"
